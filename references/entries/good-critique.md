# Critique against the goal

A critique judges work against what it is for. It starts from the goal, says what serves the goal and what doesn’t, and only then suggests a fix, so the designer gets something to act on and still chooses the fix.

That is what separates it from a reaction: “I don’t like it” says how you feel, not what to change. Put needs like access into the goal too, such as “works with a keyboard alone”, so a missing focus ring counts against the goal and nobody can wave it off as polish.

## Kinds of critique

Work at different stages needs different feedback, so there is one kind of critique for each stage. Each kind wastes time when it comes at the wrong stage, so [ask which one is wanted](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-3). If you can’t ask, go by how finished the work is.

Table: https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-kinds-table

| Kind | When | Look at |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Early sketches, while the idea can still change | Whether it solves the right problem |
| Flow | The steps exist as rough screens | Whether people can get from start to finish without getting stuck |
| Polish | The flow is settled and the screens are close to done | Contrast, alignment and wording |

- **Direction.** Two early sketches of a shopping list.
- **Flow.** Three rough screens, from a recipe to the list.
- **Polish.** A shopping list that’s close to done.

## Rules

Four rules keep critique useful. Each comes with a demo. Switch between “Common mistake” and “Do this instead” to see the difference.

### 1. Tie each comment to the goal

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-1

“I don’t like it” leaves the designer guessing, so they either defend the design or change something at random. Say what the design does, or fails to do, for the people it’s for. A reaction is still useful as a clue: look closer at what caused it, then name that.

### 2. Name the problem before the fix

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-2

“Make the button bigger” is a fix without a problem. The designer can only do it or argue, and the fix may not solve anything. Name the problem first, such as “people can’t find Checkout”, so the designer can judge your fix and may find a better one.

### 3. Ask what feedback is wanted first

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-3

Notes on spacing and colors don’t help an early sketch, because the idea may still change and take the details with it. Ask first whether the designer wants feedback on direction, flow or polish. For early work, ask about the idea and leave the details for later.

### 4. Hear their read first

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-4

When the reviewer speaks first, the designer’s view bends toward theirs: they defend the design or take every note, and the doubt they already had never comes up. Ask the designer what works for them and what worries them, then give your critique, and say where the two agree and where they don’t. The same goes for an agent’s review: get the designer’s read before you show it.

## In real products

Notice that each one ties feedback to a goal or to one spot, and leaves the fix to the owner.

- Grammarly: you set goals such as audience and formality first. Each suggestion then names the problem before it offers a rewrite, and you can accept or dismiss it. `good-critique-example-01`
- Figma: a comment is pinned to the exact spot on the canvas, so feedback is about one specific thing. The thread is resolved once it’s dealt with. `good-critique-example-02`
- GitHub: a review comment sits on the exact line and can carry a suggested change. The reviewer explains the problem, and the author decides whether to commit the fix. `good-critique-example-03`
- Airbnb: guests rate cleanliness, accuracy, check-in, communication, location and value separately. A host learns what to fix, not just that the stay got four stars. `good-critique-example-04`
