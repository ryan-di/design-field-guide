# Decision matrix

A decision matrix scores each direction from 1 to 5 against each criterion in the brief. Each criterion gets a weight, so the things the problem is about count more. The weighted total starts the decision, but it doesn’t make it.

A score of 4 against a 3 is a judgment, not a measurement. Write one line of why next to each score, so a reviewer can argue with the reason instead of the number.

## Rules

Four rules keep a decision matrix useful.

### 1. Score against the brief, not the polish

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#eff-decision-matrix-rule-1

Agents make every direction look finished, and the most polished one draws the eye, but polish says little about whether it solves the problem. Score every direction against the brief’s criteria before you pick.

### 2. Set the weights before you score

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#eff-decision-matrix-rule-2

Once you’ve seen the scores, it’s easy to nudge the weights until your favorite wins, and the matrix only confirms what you already wanted. Write the weights down from the brief first, then score on your own before you read anyone else’s scores, an agent’s included, so [their read doesn’t become yours](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-critique-rule-4).

### 3. Use the total to start the decision

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#eff-decision-matrix-rule-3

A total of 24 against 23 looks like an answer, but the numbers are judgments. If one small change to a weight flips the winner, the directions are close, so decide on the risk you care about most and say which one it is.

### 4. Keep three to five criteria

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#eff-decision-matrix-rule-4

With many criteria all weighted the same, the one the problem is about counts as little as “looks modern”, so a direction can win while failing the problem. Keep three to five criteria from the brief, and weight the ones the problem is about higher.

## In real products

- Intercom: its RICE method scores each idea by reach, impact, confidence and effort. Confidence pulls down scores that rest on guesses, and the score is meant to inform the call, not make it. `eff-decision-matrix-example-01`
- Aha!: a team sets up a scorecard once, with its criteria and an equation for how much each counts. Every idea is then rated on the same card, so scores compare like with like. `eff-decision-matrix-example-02`
- Google Flights: the top results are ranked by a mix of price and convenience, and each row shows the price, duration and stops. If price matters most to you, you re-sort by price. `eff-decision-matrix-example-03`
