Dieter Rams designed radios, record players and shelves for Braun and Vitsœ. His ten principles of good design are still among the most quoted in design. He summed them up as “Less, but better.”

He wrote them about physical products, and in software they work best as questions to ask of a screen. The readings in the cards below are one translation, so argue with them and write your own.

## Rules

Three rules keep the principles useful. Each comes with a demo. Switch between “Common mistake” and “Do this instead” to see the difference.

### 1. Ask them as questions, not rules

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-rams-principles-rule-1

Read as rules, some of the ten pull against each other. “Unobtrusive” says hide the toolbar, “understandable” says label every tool, and a checklist can’t say which one wins. Ask each one as a question about the goal, and the tension leads you to a design that serves both.

### 2. Cut what doesn’t serve the goal

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-rams-principles-rule-2

“As little design as possible” is often read as minimal styling: gray, flat, no labels. The screen looks simple, but people have to hunt for what they need. Remove what doesn’t serve the goal instead, and keep a visible label or a clear button, because it is often the essential part.

### 3. Treat “honest” as a design rule

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-rams-principles-rule-3

“Honest” sounds like a rule for advertising, so teams skip it, and a sign-up gets a fake countdown, a pre-ticked add-on and a price that grows later. People find out after they pay and stop trusting the product. Show the full cost before people commit, and leave add-ons unticked. See [Smart defaults](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#pat-smart-defaults).

## In real products

Each one serves a principle by leaving something out, or by caring about a detail most people never notice.

- Notion: a new page is a title and an empty line. Every kind of block waits behind the / key, so the page stays plain until you need more. As little design as possible. `good-rams-principles-example-01`
- Photoshop: press Tab and the panels hide, leaving only the image. Press it again and they come back. The tool steps aside for the work. Unobtrusive. `good-rams-principles-example-02`
- App Store: each app’s page shows a privacy summary of the data it collects, before you download it. People see the cost up front. Honest. `good-rams-principles-example-03`
- iOS: drag past the top or bottom of a list and the content moves a little further, then springs back when you let go. It tells you that you reached the end, not that the app froze. Thorough down to the last detail. `good-rams-principles-example-04`
