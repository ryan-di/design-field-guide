# Plain language

Design Field Guide v35 (2026-11) · Part 1 · Good design · Chapter 4 · Treat the words as the interface
Live: https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-plain-language
Kind: principle
Cite as: Design Field Guide v35, #good-plain-language

> Evidence boundary: this is a learning guide. Its demos and product examples are illustrations written for the book, not verified product documentation, so check a product’s own docs before citing how it behaves. Tools named in it are 2026 examples, not endorsements.

Plain language means the words people already use, in short sentences, with the main point first and one name for each concept. People read fast and often read only part of the text, so plain words let them understand it the first time.

It helps everyone, and most of all people reading in a second language, people with cognitive or learning disabilities, and anyone tired or stressed. [Labels](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-labels) apply the same rules to buttons.

## Rules

Four rules keep writing plain.

### 1. Use everyday words

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-plain-language-rule-1

Words like “utilize” and “initiate” make people slow down and translate. Some stop reading, and people reading in a second language may not know them at all. Use the words people use, like “use” and “start”. If a technical term can’t be avoided, explain it the first time it appears, including where to find it.

### 2. Put the point first

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-plain-language-rule-2

When a message opens with background and conditions, people have to read to the end to learn what it means for them, and many stop before they get there. A notification preview may show only the first line. Lead with what people need to know or do, and put the reason and the details after it.

### 3. One idea per sentence

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-plain-language-rule-3

A long sentence makes people hold several ideas at once, so they lose track, read it again, or miss the part that mattered to them. Give each idea its own sentence, and split any sentence that needs “and” twice.

### 4. Give each thing one name

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-plain-language-rule-4

If a shop says “bag” in one place, “basket” in another and “cart” in a third, people wonder whether these are different places and look for their items in the wrong one. Pick one term, keep it in a short glossary, and use it everywhere, help pages included, as [one thing, one name](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-consistency) explains.

## In real products

- Google Docs: the menu, the side panel and the button all use the same word: Version history, Name current version, Restore this version. `good-plain-language-example-01`
- Uber: the main field asks “Where to?”, the way a driver would, instead of “Enter destination address”. Saved places use people’s own names, like Home and Work. `good-plain-language-example-02`
- iOS: a setting has a plain name, and the line under it says what it does with everyday examples. Low Power Mode names what it cuts back, like downloads and mail, and for how long. `good-plain-language-example-03`
