You train taste by looking closely at good work. Rebuild a screen you admire, detail by detail, and name why each detail is there. Copying makes you notice spacing, weight and color that you would otherwise skim past.

Looking closely is not the same as collecting: a folder of screenshots trains nothing until you compare, rebuild and explain what you saved.

## Rules

Four rules make close looking pay off.

### 1. Tag what you save by problem

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-train-your-eye-rule-1

Screenshots saved under their default names are hard to find again, so when you design an empty state next month, you can’t search for one. Tag each shot by the problem it solves, like “empty state”, “bulk delete” or “long names”, so your collection answers the question you have.

### 2. Write down why it works

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-train-your-eye-rule-2

A copy without a reason only fits the case you copied, so when the next case is different, you don’t know which part to keep. Write one line next to each copy on why it works, because the reason still helps when the picture doesn’t match.

### 3. Study whole flows, not showcase shots

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-train-your-eye-rule-3

Showcase shots show only the happy path with perfect data, and much of the design work goes into [the other states](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-needs-hierarchy). Sign up, make mistakes on purpose, and capture every state you see. Tab through the flow and turn on a screen reader for a minute too.

### 4. Compare one property at a time

https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#good-train-your-eye-rule-4

Looking at two whole screens, you only notice that one “feels nicer”, and you can’t use what you can’t name. Compare one property at a time: spacing, then weight, then color, then corners. Small differences are easy to see once you look for one thing.

## In real products

- Mobbin: a library of screenshots from real apps, filed by app, by flow and by pattern such as empty states. You can line up how many products answered the same problem. `good-train-your-eye-example-01`
- Figma: select a layer and hold Option (Alt on Windows) while pointing at another to see the distance between them. Copying a design becomes measuring it, not guessing. `good-train-your-eye-example-02`
- Chrome DevTools: inspect any element on a live site to read its exact spacing, font and colors, then change a value in place to see why it was chosen. `good-train-your-eye-example-03`
- Pinterest: you save each image to a board you name. A board named for a problem, like “Empty states”, becomes a reference you can open when that problem comes up. `good-train-your-eye-example-04`
