# Chapter 15 · Lean on how people already behave

Design Field Guide v35 (2026-11) · Part 2 · Patterns
Live: https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#ch-laws
Cite as: Design Field Guide v35, #ch-laws

> Evidence boundary: this is a learning guide. Its demos and product examples are illustrations written for the book, not verified product documentation, so check a product’s own docs before citing how it behaves. Tools named in it are 2026 examples, not endorsements.

Some designs feel right before anyone can say why. Often it’s because they lean on a few well-known observations about people: what they expect, how they point, how they choose. Jon Yablonski collected many of them at lawsofux.com. Treat them as rules of thumb: they explain a lot and help you spot problems, but they still need checking with real people.

## Entries

- [Jakob’s law](../entries/pat-jakobs-law.md): Jakob’s law says that people spend most of their time in other products, so they expect yours to work the same way.
- [Fitts’s law](../entries/pat-fitts-law.md): Fitts’s law says that the time it takes to hit a target depends on its size and its distance.
- [Hick’s law](../entries/pat-hicks-law.md): Hick’s law says that the more choices people see, the longer it takes them to pick one, because each extra option is one more thing to read and compare.
- [Tesler’s law](../entries/pat-teslers-law.md): Tesler’s law says every task holds some complexity that can’t be removed, only moved.
