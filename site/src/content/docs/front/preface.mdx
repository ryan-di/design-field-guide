This book is for anyone who decides how an interface should work, and most of all for design engineers, who make those calls in code. It teaches by showing: every idea comes with a demo you can try on the page.

## Three ways to read it

Read it from the start. Each part builds on the one before, and Part 4 puts the first three to work on one problem. Or start from a question: [When you hear this, try that](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#ch-patterns-cheat) goes from what people say to a pattern, and [Before you pick one, ask three questions](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#ch-choose) goes from what people need to a component. Or keep it open while you work: [Before you ship, ask these](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#ch-good-checklist) and [Effective design, step by step](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/#ch-eff-checklist) turn the book into questions for your next change.

## How an entry works

An entry starts with what the idea is and a demo, with a short “Try it” line that says what to look for. Many entries then show the forms or ways it comes in. Then come the rules, each with its own demo and a switch between two sides: what goes wrong, and “Do this instead”. The first side is called “Common mistake” in Parts 1 and 4, “When it backfires” for patterns, because every pattern has a cost, and “When it breaks” for components. Last, “In real products” shows the idea in products you may know.

Tick “Learned” when an idea clicks. The contents show how many you’ve ticked, and your progress stays in this browser.

## What the examples are

The product examples are this book’s summaries of how those products worked when it was written, not the companies’ own descriptions, so check the product before you cite how it behaves. Part 4 names the tools of 2026, such as Claude Code, Figma, Linear and Playwright. Each rule says what to do first, so it still holds when a tool changes.

## How to cite it

Every entry, section and rule has its own link: press the # next to its title. To cite one, give the version and the link’s anchor, such as “Design Field Guide v35, #good-problem-framing-rule-2”. Agents can read the same book as plain text, starting from [llms.txt](https://ryan-di.github.io/design-field-guide/llms.txt).
