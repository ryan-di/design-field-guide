1. Ask: Does it make something newly possible, or newly easy?

    In software, this means new technology earns its place by removing friction people already feel.

    Example: a photo editor shows the result while you drag a slider, so people stop guessing and undoing.

  2. Ask: Does it help someone finish a real task?

    In software, this means each feature serves a job people actually have, and the screen puts that job first.

    Example: a delivery app opens on where your order is, not on offers.

  3. Ask: Is it pleasant to work in for hours?

    In software, this means calm, well-made visuals, because people who spend all day in a tool feel its quality.

    Example: a photo app keeps its own colors quiet, so people’s photos are the most colorful thing on screen.

  4. Ask: Can people tell what it does without help?

    In software, this means labels, structure and feedback explain each part, so the interface teaches itself.

    Example: a date field says what it expects, like “Day / Month / Year”, before people type.

  5. Ask: Does it step back so people’s own work can lead?

    In software, this means few interruptions, and controls that stay quiet until people need them.

    Example: a reading app hides its toolbars while people read, and shows no pop-ups in the middle of a chapter.

  6. Ask: Does it promise only what it delivers?

    In software, this means no dark patterns: no fake urgency, no hidden costs, and no tricks that push people toward choices they wouldn’t make.

    Example: a booking site shows the full price, fees included, before people press Book.

  7. Ask: Will this still make sense in a few years?

    In software, this means patterns and formats that age well, so people’s work and habits survive each update.

    Example: documents saved years ago still open after an update, and shortcuts keep their meaning.

  8. Ask: Did anyone design the edge cases?

    In software, this means empty states, errors, long names, slow networks and the keyboard path are all designed on purpose.

    Example: a very long file name is shortened in the middle and shown in full on hover.

  9. Ask: Does it waste energy, compute or attention?

    In software, this means treating compute, bandwidth and people’s attention as limited resources.

    Example: a photo backup uploads only new photos, and waits for Wi-Fi before it sends videos.

  10. Ask: What can we remove without hurting the goal?

    In software, this means cutting what doesn’t serve the goal until only the essential is left. A clear label can be the essential part.

    Example: one Send button that always works the same way, in place of three ways to send a message.
